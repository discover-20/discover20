The 20-Month Window

Catching developmental delay in the clinic — before the door closes

The talk has one argument, and every slide serves it: growth monitoring without development monitoring is half a check-up. This room already measures the body with discipline — centiles, curves, cut-offs, action thresholds. It does not measure the brain the same way. The cost is a national picture where 84% of Egyptian children with autism are already moderate-to-severe by the time anyone names it.

ACT 1

The child you saw last Tuesday

4 min · slides 1–4

Open on a patient, not on a definition. The room should feel implicated within 90 seconds — this is a child they have already seen and already discharged.

01

Title

“The 20-Month Window — catching developmental delay before the door closes.” Your name, credentials, clinic affiliations. Teal mark only; no stock photo of a smiling baby.

02
Live vote

Twenty months old. What do you do today?

One slide, one child, four options. Read it aloud slowly:

Boy, 20 months. Weight and length on the 50th centile. Eats four foods and gags on new textures. No words. Doesn’t point. Mother says: “His uncle spoke late too.”

  • A. Reassure — reassess at 24 months
  • B. Advise on feeding, review in 3 months
  • C. Formal developmental screen today
  • D. Direct referral to developmental pediatrics

Collect by show of hands or a QR poll. Don’t answer it — say “hold that,” and return to it on slide 34.

03
Egypt data

We are not missing autism. We are finding it late.

The national picture, from 41,640 Egyptian children in 22,026 households: prevalence 1.1%, boys 1.7% vs girls 0.4%. Then the number that should land hardest —

At identification: 39% severe, 45% moderate, 16% mild. Eighty-four percent were already moderate-to-severe when someone finally wrote it down.

Say the line plainly: a screening system that mostly finds severe cases is not a screening system. It’s a waiting room.

04

What you’ll leave with

Three promises, and keep all three:

  • The developmental equivalent of a growth chart — what to check, at what visit
  • Four red flags that mean referral today, not review in three months
  • A screening flow that fits a real Egyptian clinic visit, not a research protocol
ACT 2

The brain you are already feeding

5 min · slides 5–9

This is what earns a developmental talk its place at a nutrition conference. Enter through the audience’s own expertise — don’t apologise for the topic, claim it.

05

Why this talk is on a nutrition programme

You have spent your careers building this organ. Nobody in this building is better placed to notice when it isn’t working.

The nutrition clinic sees these children earlier and more often than the developmental clinic ever will — because feeding is what brings the mother in first.

06
Visual anchor

The first 1000 days: the build curve

One curve, drawn like a growth chart because that is exactly the point. Synaptogenesis peaking in the first two years, myelination, pruning. Roughly 80% of adult brain volume laid down by age 3.

Overlay the well-child visit schedule on the same axis. The visual argument: your appointments sit directly on top of the steepest part of the curve.

07

The substrate — and what its absence looks like from across the room

Two columns. Left: iron, DHA, iodine, zinc, choline, B12, vitamin D. Right: not lab values — behaviours. Reduced alertness, blunted social reciprocity, poor attention, slowed language.

The bridge line: the same child who is nutritionally depleted will look developmentally delayed — and the same child who is developmentally delayed will become nutritionally depleted. You cannot tell which direction you are looking at without measuring both.

08
Evidence

Iron: the window that closes

Iron deficiency in the first 1000 days as the worked example the room already believes — deficits in the developing brain that correction after the window does not fully reverse. Cite the Proceedings of the Nutrition Society review.

Then generalise the shape of the argument: this is the structure of every point in this talk. Timing is the active ingredient.

09

The pivot

You measure the body with a chart, a centile, a cut-off and an action. You measure the brain by asking the mother if she’s worried. Let’s fix the second half.

Nothing else on this slide. Let it sit for two beats.

ACT 3

Surveillance is not screening

8 min · slides 10–16

The clinical core. This is the section people photograph — build it so it survives being screenshotted out of context.

10

Two different jobs, done at different times

Surveillance — every visit, every child, longitudinal, informal. Screening — scheduled visits, every child, a validated instrument, a score, a threshold.

Most clinicians believe they are screening. They are doing neither well. Say it without accusation — it’s a systems failure, not a competence failure.

11

The five components of real surveillance

From the AAP 2020 policy statement — elicit and attend to parental concerns; maintain a developmental history; make accurate observations; identify risk and protective factors; document the process and findings.

Emphasis on the first: parental concern is a screening instrument with published sensitivity. “I’m worried about his speech” is a positive result, not small talk.

12
Take-home slide

The schedule — the developmental equivalent of a vaccine card

Design this as a single clean timeline; it’s the most-photographed slide in the deck.

  • 9, 18, 30 months — general developmental screening, validated tool
  • 18 and 24 months — autism-specific screening, both visits
  • Every visit in between — surveillance
  • Any visit, any age — screen whenever a concern surfaces, from anyone

Note the 24-month screen catches children who regress after a normal 18-month result. That is why it is two screens and not one.

13
Verify before use

Why clinical judgment alone is not enough

The argument that unaided clinical impression detects a minority of developmental problems, while a validated instrument detects most of them.

Check this one. The commonly quoted “~30% without a tool vs ~70–80% with” figures come from older primary-care literature. Pull the current source and cite it exactly, or drop the percentages and make the point qualitatively. A nutrition-conference audience will not challenge it — but if one person does, you want the citation on the slide.
14
Red flagsTake-home slide

Four absolute indications — refer today

  • No babbling, pointing or other gesture by 12 months
  • No single words by 16 months
  • No two-word spontaneous phrases by 24 months
  • Any loss of language or social skill, at any age

The fourth is the one to dwell on. Regression is never a variant of normal and never warrants watchful waiting.

15
Case + vote

Case 2 — the “boys talk late” trap

Same 20-month-old from slide 2, plus the family history. Ask the room what the uncle’s late speech actually changes about management.

The answer: it raises the prior probability of a genuine problem — familial clustering of language and neurodevelopmental disorders is real. Family history is a risk factor. It has been repurposed in clinic as a reassurance. That inversion is where the years go.

16

“Wait and see” is a prescription — and it has a dose

Three months of watchful waiting at 20 months is not neutral observation. It is three months withheld from the steepest part of the curve on slide 6.

Write the dose on the slide: a 3-month review at this age costs roughly 12 weeks of peak-plasticity intervention time. Frame it the way you would frame delaying iron for a child with a haemoglobin of 8.

ACT 4

Autism and ADHD: what the window is worth

8 min · slides 17–23

The emotional and evidential peak. Prevalence establishes stakes, the ESDM trial proves the window is real, and the ADHD slide stops the talk from being an autism talk.

17
Prevalence

How common is this in your waiting room?

  • 1 in 31 US 8-year-olds (CDC ADDM, 2022 surveillance year) — 3.2%, up steeply across successive cohorts
  • 1.1% in Egypt, ages 1–12, national household survey
  • Boys ~4× girls in the Egyptian data (1.7% vs 0.4%)

Handle the gap honestly: the Egyptian figure is lower partly because it is a different method and a different age band — and partly because ascertainment is a mirror. A country finds the children it screens for.

Anticipate the question. Someone will ask whether autism is “really increasing.” Have one prepared sentence: broadened criteria, better ascertainment and true increase are all in the mix, and disentangling them doesn’t change your clinical obligation to the child in front of you. Don’t litigate it from the podium.
18
Visual anchor

The gap

The single most powerful graphic in the deck: one horizontal timeline, two markers.

  • Mother’s first concern — typically before 24 months
  • Diagnosis — commonly around age 4, later in much of the region

Shade the space between them and label it plainly: the lost years. Then: “That gap is not a queue problem. Most of it happens inside our clinics, in three-month increments.”

Source the local number. Use an Egyptian or MENA age-at-diagnosis figure here rather than a US one — the room will feel it far more. If you can’t source one cleanly, say “commonly reported around age 4 internationally; there is no good Egyptian figure, which is itself the finding.”
19

Why the window is real

Short and mechanistic. Experience-dependent plasticity is highest when the social circuits are still being wired. Intervention during that period changes trajectory; the same intervention later changes function. Both are worth doing — they are not worth the same.

20
Headline evidence

What two years bought — the ESDM trial

Dawson et al., Pediatrics 2010. 48 toddlers, randomised, entered at 18–30 months, two years of intervention.

  • ESDM group gained 17.6 standard score points; community-treatment group gained 7.0
  • Adaptive behaviour maintained pace with typically developing peers; the comparison group fell further behind
  • Children in the ESDM arm were more likely to shift diagnostic classification from autism to PDD-NOS

Land it: a ten-point difference, purchased with two years that began at eighteen months. Not with a drug. With time you can only spend once.

21

And it was visible in the brain

The follow-up EEG work — early behavioural intervention associated with normalised patterns of brain activity to social stimuli. One line, one image. This is the slide that converts the sceptics in the room.

22
Don’t skip

ADHD: caught at seven, visible at three

Deliberately different in tone — this is where the room’s own children live too.

Why it presents late: symptoms are read as temperament until school imposes a structure the child fails against. Preschool signals worth logging — extreme and persistent activity beyond the age norm, minimal sustained engagement in any activity, aggression and expulsion from nursery, sleep and feeding dysregulation.

The honest caveat, stated from the podium: you are not diagnosing ADHD at three. You are documenting a trajectory so that whoever assesses at six has four years of data instead of a parent’s memory.

Nutrition tie-in worth one line. Stimulant-related appetite suppression and growth trajectory — the reason this room ends up co-managing these children anyway.
23
Needs your input

Where do you actually send them? — the Egypt pathway

This slide is the difference between a talk people enjoyed and a talk that changes practice. A screen with nowhere to refer is a screen nobody repeats.

Give me the local map and I’ll build it: developmental pediatrics referral routes in Cairo/Giza, public vs private, realistic waiting times, early-intervention providers you would personally send a patient to, and what a family can start at home in the meantime while they wait.

ACT 6

Screens, language and the feeding table

6 min · slides 29–33

Evidence-tight framing, as you chose. The claim is displacement of early interaction — dose-response and defensible — not population IQ decline, which is contested and would hand a sceptic an easy win.

29
Live vote

How many hours?

“Your average one-year-old patient: how many hours a day is a screen on in front of them?” Options: <1 · 1–2 · 2–4 · 4+ · I have never asked.

The last option is usually the honest majority — and it sets up the closing ask.

30
Headline evidence

There is a dose-response curve

Tohoku Medical Megabank birth cohort, 7,097 mother–child pairs (JAMA Pediatrics, 2023). Screen hours at age 1, developmental delay measured at 2 and 4.

Plot it as a rising curve, not a table — communication delay at age 2:

  • 1–<2 h/day — OR 1.61 (1.23–2.10)
  • 2–<4 h/day — OR 2.04 (1.52–2.74)
  • ≥4 h/day — OR 4.78 (3.24–7.06)

Still present at age 4 (≥4 h/day: OR 2.68), and the same shape for problem-solving. Say the word association out loud once — then point out that a monotonic dose-response relationship persisting to age 4 is exactly the pattern this audience already accepts as meaningful in nutritional epidemiology.

31

The mechanism is subtraction, not toxicity

The screen is not poisoning the brain. It is occupying the hours in which serve-and-return conversation would have happened — and language is built almost entirely out of contingent, responsive turns with a human being.

A screen never waits for your child to answer. That pause is the entire curriculum.

This framing is also what makes the advice actionable: you are not asking families to fear a device, you are asking them to protect an interaction.

32
Nutrition crossover

The screen at the feeding table

Your second bridge, and the one this room will act on fastest. Screen-fed toddlers eat while distracted — interoceptive hunger and satiety cues are overridden, mealtime is decoupled from appetite, and food refusal gets solved with a tablet instead of with exposure.

The clinical ask is one question added to every feeding history: “Is anything playing while he eats?”

33
Advanced point

Feeding difficulty as a developmental sign — and screens as a confounder

Two things at once, and this is the intellectual peak of the talk for this audience.

First: children with ASD are around 5× more likely to have feeding problems (meta-analytic OR 5.11, 95% CI 3.74–6.97; 17 controlled studies), with measurably lower calcium and protein intake. Extreme food selectivity is frequently the family’s first complaint — and it arrives in a nutrition clinic, not a developmental one. You see these children before anyone else does.

Second: heavy screen exposure can produce a picture that resembles delay, and can equally mask a genuine one. The practical move is a bounded clinical trial — a structured “screen holiday” with adult interaction substituted in, and a re-screen at a defined interval. A child who catches up was displaced. A child who doesn’t was never the screen — and you have just lost no time finding out.

Say this explicitly. A screen holiday is a clarifying test, never a reason to defer referral in a child with any Act 3 red flag. Put that sentence on the slide so it cannot be misquoted.

Rules of thumb

  • One idea per slide. 35 slides in 40 minutes means roughly 70 seconds each — the deck must carry no reading load.
  • Numbers get their own slide, set large. An odds ratio buried in a bullet is a wasted odds ratio.
  • Every evidence slide carries its citation at 12pt in the bottom corner. Non-negotiable at a scientific conference.
  • No stock photography of children. Use your own clinical schematics, or nothing.

Two judgment calls I made

Both are places where I steered away from what you first described. Overrule either one and I’ll rebuild that section.

Screen time. You framed this as “our generation is less smart.” I built Act 6 on displacement of early interaction instead. The reason is practical, not squeamish: population IQ trends are genuinely contested — the reversal findings are inconsistent across cohorts and heavily confounded by test composition, sampling and education effects. In a room of physicians, one prepared sceptic could take that claim apart and the doubt would spread backwards over your prevalence and ESDM data, which are solid. The dose-response cohort evidence makes a narrower claim that nobody in that hall can dismantle — and it is the version that tells them what to do on Sunday.

Discover20’s placement. I put it at Act 5, after the friction problem is established in Act 3 and the stakes in Act 4, and gave it an explicit limits slide (28). At a scientific meeting a tool introduced early reads as a sponsor slot and the audience discounts everything after it. Introduced as the answer to a problem they have just admitted to having — and bounded honestly — it reads as a contribution. It also matches how you built the antiemetic session: clinical questions first, product as resolution.
